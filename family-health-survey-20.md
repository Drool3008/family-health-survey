# Family Health — Online Survey (22 questions)
**Study:** Managing the family's health — The Ken Case Competition 2026
**Mode:** Online, self-administered, filled separately by every member of the family
**Sample:** 5 families × up to 4 members (~20 respondents)
**Length:** 22 questions + 1 attention check. 7–9 minutes.
**Reading level:** written to be answered by a 19-year-old and a 72-year-old without help.

---

## Design rules used

- **Same form for every role.** No branching. A parent, a son, a daughter-in-law and a sibling all answer the same 20 questions. Their answers are then compared against each other — the disagreements are the finding.
- **Depth follows a bell curve.** Questions 1–5 are easy facts. 6–9 climb into behaviour. **10, 11, 12 and 13 are the peak — agency, consent, concealment and dread.** 14–22 descend back into concrete choices and end light.
- **Every question names a real thing.** No "how do you manage", no "how satisfied are you", no hypotheticals longer than one line. Older respondents drop out on abstraction, not on length.
- **4–6 options each,** mutually exclusive unless marked SELECT ALL, no option requiring the respondent to hold two ideas at once.
- **Offer the form in the respondent's language.** A parent answering Q17 about language barriers in English is a wasted response.

## B.2 The depth curve

| Position | Questions | Cognitive and emotional load |
|---|---|---|
| Opening, shallow | 1–3 | Facts about the household. No recall, no judgement. |
| Rising | 4–7 | Specific recall of one recent event, and one physical action. |
| Approaching peak | 8–9 | The first uncomfortable realisation: nobody else can do this. |
| **Peak** | **10–12** | **Agency, consent, concealment. The questions a respondent would not volunteer.** |
| **Peak** | **13** | **The dread, named. Highest amplitude in the instrument.** |
| Descending | 14–17 | Back to concrete mechanics — money, refills, phone calls. |
| Tail | 18–19 | Recognition tasks. Ticking familiar names and single steps. |
| Small second rise | 20–21 | The two wish questions. Pleasant to answer, but they carry the consent finding. |
| Close | 22 | One easy choice. Ends light. |

Q13 is placed immediately after Q12 on purpose. A respondent who has just admitted that health matters get hidden is far more likely to name their own dread honestly than one asked cold. Do not reorder these three.

## B.3 The trigger — how to read Q4

Q4 is the question the whole instrument is built around. Cross it against Q5:

- **"Person said they were feeling unwell" + 3 to 7 days** → the trigger is human report, and the delay sits in scheduling
- **"Someone else noticed" + more than a week** → the trigger is observation by a third party, and the delay is persuasion
- **"Medicine was finishing"** → the trigger is a supply clock, not a symptom. This is the one an agent can own outright
- **"Doctor asked them to come back"** → the trigger already exists in a system somewhere and still failed to reach the family
- **"Sudden emergency"** → no trigger; everything upstream failed

If most families cluster on "medicine was finishing" or "doctor asked them to come back", the case is that the trigger is already machine-readable and nobody is reading it.

## B.4 Cross-member comparison — where the real finding is

Every member of a family shares a code, so these pairs can be compared directly. The gaps matter more than the totals.

| Compare | Between whom | What a gap tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | All members | Do they even agree on who is carrying it? |
| Q8 | The person named in Q1 vs everyone else | Coordinator says "anyone could"; others say "nobody" → the load is invisible to the one carrying it |
| Q9 | Coordinator vs others | The handoff exists in theory only |
| Q10 vs Q11 | Older person vs adult child | Child says the person is fine with it; the person says they did not like it |
| Q12 vs Q13 | Older person vs adult child | The parent hides things so the child will not worry; the child's dread is "I am not being told everything". Same loop, two ends. |
| Q15 | Coordinator vs older person | Whether the money workaround the coordinator believes in actually exists at the shop |
| Q17 | Coordinator vs older person | What the child thinks goes wrong on the call vs what the parent finds hard |
| Q19 | All members | Where the family agrees to hand over, and where exactly it refuses |
| Q20 | All members | Whether the family agrees on which job is worst. If the coordinator picks one job and everyone else picks another, the family does not see the work being done |
| Q21 | Coordinator vs older person | **The sharpest test in the instrument.** The child ticking "nothing — it should come through me first" against the parent ticking four things they would happily do themselves is the consent problem in a single row |
| Q19 vs Q21 | Within one respondent | What they will delegate for themselves versus what they will allow their parent to do directly. The gap between those two answers is not convenience, it is control |

## B.5 Fielding notes

- Send individually, never to the family group. Members filling it together produce one sanitised account and destroy the comparison.
- One question per screen for Q10, Q11, Q12. Multiple deep questions on a single screen get straight-lined.
- Randomise option order in Q4, Q6, Q13, Q16, Q17, Q19, Q20, Q21, Q22. Keep "None", "Nobody" and "I don't know" pinned last.
- Force a response on all 22. Leave only the free-text box skippable.
- Exclude anyone failing the attention check. Report respondents fielded, respondents retained, and families with at least three members responding.
- Target at least one older respondent per family. Q10, Q11, Q12 and Q17 lose most of their value if only adult children answer them.
